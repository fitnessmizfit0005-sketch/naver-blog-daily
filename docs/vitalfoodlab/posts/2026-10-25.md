<p>A blood pressure monitor shows 138/76, and the first question is often, "Is that normal for my age?" The answer matters because a number can be common among older adults without being a healthy target. Getting older does not automatically move a high reading into the normal category.</p>

<p>This guide to normal blood pressure by age explains the current US categories, what age-related statistics actually tell us, and how to get useful readings at home. It also separates a treatment goal from a diagnosis and explains when an unusually high result needs immediate attention.</p>

<h2>Read the Two Numbers Before Looking at Age</h2>
<p>Systolic pressure, the upper number, measures pressure as the heart contracts. Diastolic pressure, the lower number, measures pressure between beats. Both are reported in millimeters of mercury, or mm Hg. Under the 2025 AHA/ACC framework, the adult categories are:</p>
<table><thead><tr><th>Category</th><th>Systolic, mm Hg</th><th>Diastolic, mm Hg</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Normal</td><td>Below 120</td><td>And below 80</td></tr><tr><td>Elevated</td><td>120–129</td><td>And below 80</td></tr><tr><td>Stage 1 hypertension</td><td>130–139</td><td>Or 80–89</td></tr><tr><td>Stage 2 hypertension</td><td>140 or higher</td><td>Or 90 or higher</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Use the higher category when the numbers disagree. For example, 138/76 falls in stage 1, while 118/84 also falls in stage 1 because of the lower number. A single reading generally does not establish a diagnosis; a clinician reviews repeated measurements and the circumstances in which they were taken.</p>

<h2>What Changes With Age, and What Does Not</h2>
<p>Arteries tend to become stiffer with age, helping explain why systolic pressure often rises. But there is no AHA/ACC chart that assigns a progressively higher "normal" value to each decade of adult life. Population averages describe what researchers measured, including people with untreated or treated hypertension. They are not recommended targets.</p>
<p>A 2024 National Center for Health Statistics report using US survey data from August 2021 through August 2023 illustrates the age pattern. These are hypertension prevalence figures, not average blood pressure readings:</p>
<table><thead><tr><th>Adult age group</th><th>Percentage with hypertension</th><th>How to interpret it</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>18–39</td><td>23.4%</td><td>Young adults can have hypertension, too</td></tr><tr><td>40–59</td><td>52.5%</td><td>High pressure becomes more common in midlife</td></tr><tr><td>60 and older</td><td>71.6%</td><td>Common does not mean harmless or normal</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>The report counted adults with pressure at least 130 systolic or 80 diastolic, or those taking blood pressure medication. It therefore includes some people whose current readings were controlled by treatment. The survey describes a population; it cannot tell an individual which medication or target is appropriate.</p>

<h2>Why Treatment Goals Need a Separate Conversation</h2>
<p>The 2025 guideline gives an overarching treatment goal below 130/80 for adults, with special considerations including institutional care, limited life expectancy, and pregnancy. That is different from the definition of normal pressure below 120/80. Your care plan also considers symptoms and how well treatment is tolerated.</p>
<p>In the 2015 SPRINT trial, 9,361 adults at increased cardiovascular risk without diabetes were assigned to systolic targets below 120 or below 140. Intensive treatment reduced major cardiovascular events, but some serious adverse effects, including kidney injury and low blood pressure, were more frequent.</p>
<p>A 2016 analysis of 2,636 SPRINT participants aged 75 or older also found fewer major cardiovascular events and deaths with intensive treatment. This was an analysis of the same trial, not independent confirmation in a new population. It supports discussing effective treatment in later life, rather than accepting high readings simply because of age. It does not justify adjusting medication yourself.</p>

<h2>Make Home Readings Worth Comparing</h2>
<p>A hurried measurement after stairs or coffee can create confusion. The American Heart Association recommends an automatic, validated upper-arm monitor with a correctly sized cuff. Bring it to an appointment so your team can check your technique and compare equipment.</p>
<ol><li>Avoid smoking, caffeine, and exercise for 30 minutes beforehand, and empty your bladder.</li><li>Sit quietly for at least five minutes. Support your back, keep feet flat, and leave legs uncrossed.</li><li>Place the cuff on bare skin. Support your arm so the cuff is at heart level.</li><li>Stay silent and still. Take two readings one minute apart.</li><li>Record both results and the time. Follow your clinician's schedule for how often and how many days to measure.</li></ol>
<p>Share the record rather than selecting only the lowest result. Differences between home and office readings can prompt further assessment, including ambulatory monitoring. A smartwatch reading should not replace validated cuff measurements used for diagnosis.</p>

<h2>Look for Patterns You Can Act On</h2>
<p>Consistently high pressure can reflect several influences, including family history, excess sodium, alcohol, inactivity, and underlying health conditions. A clinician may also review prescriptions, over-the-counter products, and possible contributing conditions. Bring a complete medication list rather than stopping something on your own.</p>
<p>The practical foundation is a DASH-style eating pattern, less sodium, regular activity, appropriate weight management, and reducing or avoiding alcohol. For food choices that support that approach, see our guide to <a href="https://vitalfoodlab.com/best-foods-to-lower-blood-pressure/">foods to lower blood pressure</a>. Food changes complement treatment; they do not make follow-up unnecessary.</p>
<p>Medication decisions use average readings and cardiovascular risk. Under the 2025 guidance, even lower-risk adults with persistent stage 1 hypertension may need medication after a three- to six-month lifestyle trial. Agree on a review date so an initial lifestyle plan does not become indefinite waiting.</p>

<h2>Know Which Readings Cannot Wait</h2>
<p>For a nonpregnant adult, pressure higher than 180 systolic and/or 120 diastolic needs urgent attention. If there are no concerning symptoms, wait at least one minute and repeat it. If it remains that high, contact your healthcare professional as soon as possible.</p>
<p><strong>Call 911 immediately if a very high reading comes with chest pain, shortness of breath, weakness, numbness, vision changes, back pain, or trouble speaking.</strong> Do not delay emergency care to keep rechecking. Possible stroke or heart attack symptoms warrant emergency help even without a blood pressure measurement. Pregnancy requires separate guidance; do not use this adult chart as a pregnancy safety threshold.</p>

<h2>Questions About Your Blood Pressure Numbers</h2>
<h3>Is 140/90 normal at age 70?</h3>
<p>No. It meets the US adult stage 2 category. The treatment plan may be individualized, but age alone does not make that reading normal.</p>
<h3>Can I have high blood pressure and feel fine?</h3>
<p>Yes. Hypertension usually has no symptoms. Regular measurement is more useful than waiting for a headache or a change in how you feel.</p>
<h3>Should I stop medicine once home readings improve?</h3>
<p>No. Improved readings may mean treatment is working. Share the log and any dizziness or other symptoms with your prescriber before changing doses.</p>
